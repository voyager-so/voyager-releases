# Make a mobile drift game with three.js in Voyager

Build Petit Rallye, a portrait three.js drift game for phones, in one Voyager conversation: thumb steering, a random road, Paris shops, phone testing and demo videos.

Source: https://voyager.so/blog/mobile-drift-game-threejs

We made **Petit Rallye**, a drift game for phones, in one Voyager conversation. You hold anywhere on the screen and slide your thumb to throw a little red Mini through an endless Paris street. The road bends at random, red and white curbs mark the inside of each turn, and pastel boulangeries and cafés slide past on both sides.

It's a three.js game that runs in the phone's browser, with no app store and no install. This post walks through the brief, testing it on a phone, the small changes that made it feel right and the demo videos.

## What you need

* **Voyager** with the `make-game` skill.
* **A phone** on the same Wi-Fi as your computer, for testing with your thumb.
* **No credits.** The art is built in code and the sounds are generated in the browser, so nothing in this build is a paid generation.

## Describe how it should feel in your hand

This is the brief we started with:

```text
Using three.js, let's create a portrait mode rally game for mobile
where you control the car drifting and turning with your thumb, and
the direction changes randomly as you go. The car should look like
a cute compact Mini Cooper, and the roads should have red and white
curbs on the side you're supposed to turn toward. The look should be
a simple, cute, cel-shaded anime style, and the buildings that pass
by should look like cute shops and boutiques you'd find in Paris.
As your thumb goes right, the car turns left, and so on.
```

Each line does a job. "Portrait mode" and "with your thumb" set the screen and the control before any art exists. "Changes randomly" means every run is a new road, so there's no level to design. The curbs are a gameplay cue as much as decoration: they tell you which way the next corner goes. The look gets three references (a Mini, cel-shaded anime, Paris boutiques) instead of a pile of adjectives.

The last line is an unusual choice, and spelling it out mattered. Sliding right to turn left feels like pushing the back of the car around, and Voyager kept it as the default with a switch to normal steering on the title screen.

## What Voyager built

Voyager set the game up as a Vite and three.js project that builds into one self-contained HTML file:

* **The road:** a generator that lays out random lefts, rights, S-bends and the odd hairpin. Corners get tighter and the car gets faster the further you go, and the curbs appear only on the inside of each turn.
* **Drifting:** past about a third of full lock the rear lets go. You get tire smoke, skid marks and a drift chain from ×1 to ×5 that pays out with "SUPER DRIFT" and "MAGNIFIQUE".
* **Pace notes:** a rally-style call such as "LEFT 3" or "HAIRPIN" warns you about the next corner. For the first three corners it also tells you which way to move your thumb.
* **The look:** cel shading with dark outlines, a chubby red Mini with a white roof and a #7 roundel, pastel Haussmann buildings with iron balconies and flower boxes, and striped awnings over a Boulangerie, Café, Fleuriste, Pâtisserie, Librairie, Fromagerie, Chocolat or Boutique. A lilac Eiffel Tower sits on the horizon.
* **Sound:** the engine, tire screech, curb rumble, chimes and crash are all generated in the browser. Android phones vibrate too.

Before handing it over, Voyager played it in a browser with an automated driver: about 800 meters in a minute at a steady frame rate, plus a deliberate crash to check the results screen. The bot's twitchy steering never banked a drift, which is the kind of thing only a real thumb can judge.

## Play it on your phone

Voyager runs the game on a local server and gives you two links: one for your computer and one with your computer's network address for a phone on the same Wi-Fi. Open the second one on your phone and play. After each change, refresh the page to get the new version.

Feel is the whole game here, and you can only judge it with your thumb on the glass. Play a few runs between requests.

## Tune it one message at a time

Every change after the first build was a single short message, written right after playing a run.

The first build drew a dot and a ring under your finger, like a virtual joystick. It felt like steering a cursor instead of the car:

```text
Rather than controlling a dot, it should just be that area that the
user can touch and control the car with.
```

Clipping a lamppost ended the run, which made tight corners frustrating rather than exciting. Now a lamppost flies off with a "Bonk !" and costs you about 18% of your speed, while hitting a shop or spinning out still ends the run:

```text
Let's make the lampposts clippable. They're making this a little
too hard.
```

The crash deserved a better last frame than the chase camera:

```text
When the game ends, let's do a close-up on the car from the bottom
up in the front so it's a nice shot.
```

Voyager's first framing was too tight, so it pulled the camera back and widened the lens. The car often ends up nose-first against a shop, so the camera tries a few angles and takes the first one that's still on the street.

## Make the title screen thumb-friendly

The title screen had the most back-and-forth, and it was all about where things sit under your thumb. We asked for simpler text and a lower "Tap to start", then moved the steering switch up under the instruction card, then nudged "Tap to start" up twice. It now sits about 15% of the screen height above the bottom: easy to reach without crowding the edge.

Small layout changes like these are worth making in short messages, one at a time, checking each on the phone. "A little more up" is a complete instruction when the last change is fresh.

## Render demo videos from the game itself

For sharing, we asked for 15-second landscape and portrait videos without the HUD, and a 10-second clip of the title screen. Instead of screen recording, Voyager added a hidden capture mode to the game and rendered every frame with a scripted driver, so the videos play perfectly smoothly at 60 frames per second. It tried a few random roads and kept the one with the best mix of drifts, gas cans and a crash near the end.

```text
Let's get a landscape video demonstration of the game, without the
UI, about 15 seconds, as well as a portrait video. 1080 is fine.
```

Rendering found two fixes that also improved the game: a narrower lens on wide screens so landscape isn't fish-eyed, and a roof number that now reads the right way up from the chase camera. In the title clip, the car spent most of its time hidden behind the steering button, so Voyager re-aimed the menu camera. The videos are silent, because the game makes its sound live in the browser.

## Share one file

The finished game is a single HTML file of about 600 KB with everything inside: three.js, the code and the styles. Send it to someone, open it on any phone or put it on any web host, and it plays. A browser game made this way goes from a paragraph to something in your friends' hands in one sitting.
